# 1. Vertical Axis Turbine for Rooftops

## Compact Wind Energy Solutions for Suitable Buildings

### Overview

Urban buildings, commercial properties, institutional facilities, and industrial rooftops may offer opportunities to explore small-scale wind energy generation. Vertical-axis wind turbines (VAWTs) are one technology that can be considered for locations where their design, structural requirements, and operating characteristics suit the site.

Unlike conventional horizontal-axis turbines, vertical-axis turbines rotate around a vertical shaft. Certain designs can accept wind from different horizontal directions without needing a yaw mechanism to continually turn the rotor toward the wind.

Depending on the design, a vertical-axis turbine may use curved or helical blades, drag-based scoops, or aerodynamic lifting blades. Each design has different characteristics relating to starting torque, operating speed, efficiency, vibration, and energy production.

AeroSun Energy can help evaluate rooftop wind energy opportunities by considering the available wind, building structure, equipment location, electrical requirements, and intended application.

### How Does a Rooftop Wind Turbine Work?

1. Wind flows across the turbine's rotor.
2. The rotor converts part of the wind's kinetic energy into rotational motion.
3. A generator converts that motion into electrical energy.
4. Power electronics regulate the variable electrical output.
5. Compatible equipment supplies an approved electrical load, charges a suitable battery system, or connects to an authorised grid-interactive system.

The exact equipment depends on the turbine and the intended application. A grid-connected system requires suitable protection and approved interconnection arrangements.

### Potential Applications

**Residential buildings**
- Supplementary renewable electricity for suitable household loads.
- Demonstration installations for renewable-energy education.
- Small-scale generation where the rooftop wind resource is adequate.

**Commercial buildings**
- Renewable-energy demonstrations at offices and business premises.
- Supplementary generation for selected electrical loads.
- Integration with a broader energy-management strategy.

**Educational institutions**
- Practical demonstrations of aerodynamics and electrical generation.
- Wind-speed and power-output monitoring.
- Renewable-energy research and student projects.

**Industrial and institutional rooftops**
- Pilot installations for evaluating small wind turbine performance.
- Remote instrumentation or other compatible low-power applications.
- Renewable-energy research and technology evaluation.

### Benefits

**Compact design options**

Some vertical-axis turbine designs can be arranged within a relatively compact footprint. The actual installation area must include the complete rotor, support structure, access provisions, and safety clearances.

**Wind-direction flexibility**

Many vertical-axis designs do not require active yaw alignment. However, this does not mean they are unaffected by turbulence or changes in wind speed.

**Technology diversity**

VAWTs provide an alternative to conventional three-bladed horizontal-axis turbines for certain applications.

**Potential supplementary generation**

Where measured wind conditions are favourable, a rooftop turbine may contribute to a building's electricity supply.

**Monitoring and learning**

A properly instrumented installation can record wind speed, rotor behaviour, electrical output, and energy production.

### Important Rooftop Considerations

Rooftops often experience turbulent airflow caused by parapets, neighbouring buildings, HVAC equipment, and other obstructions. Turbulence can reduce energy production and increase mechanical fatigue.

Before installing a rooftop turbine, the following should be assessed:

- Structural capacity of the roof and supporting frame.
- Wind speeds and turbulence at the proposed rotor location.
- Dynamic loading, vibration transmission, and noise.
- Blade clearance and access for maintenance.
- Waterproofing and roof-penetration details.
- Electrical protection, earthing, and lightning protection.
- Local building requirements and any required approvals.

**A rooftop turbine should not be selected on rated power alone.** Its expected annual energy production under actual site conditions is the more useful comparison.

### Example Calculation: A 3 kW Rooftop Wind Turbine

Consider a hypothetical 3 kW turbine installed at a location with a sufficient wind resource.

Assume:

- Rated turbine capacity: 3 kW
- Illustrative capacity factor: 20%
- Hours in a year: 8,760

**Step 1: Calculate annual energy generation**

Annual energy = Rated capacity × Hours per year × Capacity factor

Annual energy = 3 kW × 8,760 hours × 0.20

**Estimated annual generation = 5,256 kWh**

Average daily generation:

5,256 ÷ 365 = **14.4 kWh per day**

This is an illustrative estimate, not a performance guarantee. A 20% capacity factor is an assumption; it must not be interpreted as a typical result for all rooftop turbines.

**Step 2: Estimate potential electricity-bill savings**

Assume that:

- The electricity tariff is ₹8 per kWh.
- All generated electricity can be used on-site.
- No additional system losses, maintenance expenses, or financing costs are included.

Annual electricity value = 5,256 × ₹8

**Illustrative annual electricity value = ₹42,048**

Actual savings could be lower because of turbine output, conversion losses, periods of low wind, maintenance, and the timing of generation relative to electricity consumption.

### Who Should Consider This Solution?

Rooftop wind may be worth evaluating for building owners, institutions, and businesses that have an exposed site with a suitable wind resource and a structurally appropriate installation location.

For many ordinary urban rooftops, solar PV may be more predictable and economical. Wind should therefore be evaluated using site measurements and a realistic energy-yield estimate before investment.

### Example Calculation: A 500 kW Industrial Wind Turbine

Consider an illustrative 500 kW turbine at an industrial site.

Assume:

- Rated capacity: 500 kW
- Illustrative capacity factor: 30%
- Hours per year: 8,760

Annual energy = 500 × 8,760 × 0.30

**Estimated annual generation = 1,314,000 kWh**

That equals **1,314 MWh or 1.314 GWh per year**.

Average daily generation:

1,314,000 ÷ 365 = approximately **3,600 kWh per day**.

The turbine is rated at 500 kW, but its average output under this assumption is:

500 × 0.30 = **150 kW average**.

This distinction is important when comparing turbine capacity with a facility's continuous electrical demand.

### Example: Estimating Electricity Value

Suppose the industrial facility can use all 1,314,000 kWh of generated electricity and the relevant avoided electricity cost is ₹7 per kWh.

Annual electricity value = 1,314,000 × ₹7

**Illustrative annual electricity value = ₹91,98,000**

This is a gross electricity-value estimate, not net profit or guaranteed savings.

It excludes:

- Turbine and foundation capital cost.
- Civil and electrical works.
- Grid-interconnection expenses.
- Land and development costs.
- Financing and insurance.
- Operations and maintenance.
- Downtime and electrical losses.
- Any energy that cannot be used or exported economically.

If only 70% of the generation offsets on-site consumption, the on-site energy offset would be:

1,314,000 × 0.70 = **919,800 kWh per year**.

At ₹7 per kWh, the illustrative value of that on-site offset would be ₹64,38,600 before costs. The remaining generation would need to be assessed under the applicable export or power-sale arrangements.

### Industrial Wind Feasibility Assessment

A meaningful industrial assessment should examine:

1. Hourly or interval-based electricity demand.

3. Turbine power curves and expected annual energy production.
4. Land availability, setbacks, access, and foundation requirements.
5. Grid-connection capacity and applicable technical requirements.
6. Power-quality and protection requirements.
7. Noise, environmental, and permitting considerations.
8. Capital cost, operating costs, financing, and expected project life.
9. Alternative energy options and sensitivity to lower-than-expected output.

### Example Calculation: Wind Energy for a Farm Irrigation System

Consider a hypothetical farm with:

- A 10 kW wind turbine.
- An illustrative capacity factor of 25%.
- An irrigation pump rated at 5.5 kW.
- Pump operating time of 6 hours per day.
- Irrigation operating days of 180 per year.

**Step 1: Estimate annual wind generation**

Annual energy = 10 × 8,760 × 0.25

**Estimated annual generation = 21,900 kWh**

**Step 2: Calculate irrigation pump consumption**

Pump energy = Pump power × Operating hours

Annual pump energy = 5.5 × 6 × 180

**Annual pump consumption = 5,940 kWh**

**Step 3: Compare generation and consumption**

Estimated annual wind generation: 21,900 kWh.

Estimated annual pump consumption: 5,940 kWh.

The theoretical annual generation is greater than the pump's annual energy requirement. However, this does not establish that the turbine can operate the pump whenever required.

Wind energy may be unavailable during a scheduled irrigation period, and the pump may draw a higher starting current than its normal operating current.

The practical system design must therefore consider:

- Wind output during irrigation hours.
- Pump motor starting and control requirements.
- Required pumping head and water flow.
- Daily and seasonal water demand.
- Battery storage, if appropriate.
- Grid or backup supply.
- Water storage as a way to decouple pumping from immediate water demand.
- Electrical conversion and distribution losses.

### Example: Potential Electricity-Cost Offset

Assume the relevant avoided electricity cost is ₹6 per kWh.

If 5,940 kWh of wind-generated electricity actually replaces electricity that would otherwise have been purchased for irrigation:

5,940 × ₹6 = **₹35,640 per year**.

This is an illustrative electricity-cost offset for the pump's annual consumption. It is not a full project-savings estimate because the cost of the turbine, installation, maintenance, and other system components has not been included.

If the wind system produces additional energy, the value of that energy depends on whether it can be used by other farm loads or exported under an applicable arrangement.

### Alternative Approach: Wind + Solar + Water Storage

For farms with suitable resources, a hybrid system may combine:

- Wind turbines.
- Solar PV modules.
- Pump controllers or compatible power converters.
- Battery storage, where justified.
- Water storage tanks or reservoirs.
- Irrigation scheduling and monitoring.
- Grid supply or backup generation, where available.

Water storage can be particularly useful because water may be pumped when renewable electricity is available and used later when irrigation is required.

However, this approach must be designed around the required daily water volume, pumping head, seasonal demand, and actual renewable-energy availability.

### Who Should Consider This Solution?

Farmers, agricultural estates, horticultural operations, livestock facilities, and irrigation operators may consider wind energy where the measured wind resource is adequate and the energy system can be matched to agricultural demand.

In areas with weak or highly turbulent winds, solar-powered pumping may be a more practical option. A comparison should be completed before selecting the technology.

# 4. Wind Resource Assessment & Planning

## Make Informed Decisions Before Investing in Wind Energy

### Overview

A wind turbine's rated capacity does not determine how much electricity it will generate at a particular location.

Actual energy production depends on the site's wind conditions, turbine design, hub height, terrain, obstacles, turbulence, availability, electrical losses, and operating characteristics.

Wind resource assessment is therefore a critical step before selecting a turbine or committing to a wind energy project.

AeroSun Energy's proposed assessment approach focuses on understanding the available resource, the intended application, and the relationship between expected energy generation and electricity demand.

The level of assessment required depends on project size, investment risk, site complexity, and the accuracy needed for the final energy-yield estimate.

### What Is Wind Resource Assessment?

Wind resource assessment is the process of evaluating the wind conditions at a potential installation site to determine whether the site is suitable for a proposed turbine or wind energy project.

It may involve:

**Wind-speed assessment**

Evaluating wind speeds over time at a relevant height and location.

**Wind-direction analysis**

Understanding prevailing wind directions and seasonal changes.

**Terrain and obstacle assessment**

Identifying hills, valleys, trees, buildings, and other features that influence the airflow.

**Turbulence assessment**

Evaluating variations in wind speed and direction that can affect energy production and turbine loading.

**Hub-height evaluation**

Assessing the wind conditions at the intended turbine hub height rather than relying solely on measurements taken near the ground.

**Turbine power-curve analysis**

Estimating output using the selected turbine's documented power curve and the site's wind-speed distribution.

**Electrical-load assessment**

Comparing potential wind generation with the intended load, grid connection, or storage arrangement.

**Preliminary project economics**

Estimating the value of generated electricity against project costs and operating requirements.

### Why Is Wind Assessment Important?

**Avoid inappropriate turbine selection**

A turbine with a high rated capacity may produce disappointing energy at a site with insufficient wind.

**Improve energy-yield estimates**

Site-specific data can help replace broad assumptions with a more realistic estimate.

**Identify installation constraints**

The assessment may reveal turbulence, access limitations, noise considerations, or structural constraints.

**Support financial decisions**

Expected annual energy production is a major input to electricity-savings and project-payback calculations.

**Compare alternative locations**

Wind conditions can vary significantly across a property. Comparing candidate locations may improve the project design.

### Our Proposed Assessment Process

**Stage 1: Initial consultation**

Understand the intended application, electrical load, project goals, site location, and preliminary budget.

**Stage 2: Site screening**

Review available wind information, terrain, obstacles, access, and potential installation locations.

**Stage 3: Measurement planning**

Determine whether existing data is adequate or whether site-specific wind measurements are required.

**Stage 4: Wind-data analysis**

Evaluate wind speed, direction, seasonal patterns, turbulence where measurable, and the relationship between wind conditions and the proposed turbine.

**Stage 5: Turbine selection**

Compare suitable turbines using their documented power curves, operating limits, installation requirements, and expected energy yield.

**Stage 6: Energy-yield estimation**

Estimate gross and net annual generation, with suitable allowances for relevant losses and uncertainty.

**Stage 7: Preliminary financial assessment**

Compare the expected energy value with indicative capital, operating, and maintenance costs.

**Stage 8: Project planning**

Identify the next engineering, structural, environmental, electrical, and regulatory steps.

The exact scope and deliverables should be agreed upon for each project. A preliminary desktop screening is not the same as a bankable wind-resource assessment.

### Example Calculation: Annual Energy Production

Suppose a proposed turbine has:

- Rated capacity: 100 kW.
- Illustrative capacity factor: 30%.
- Annual operating period: 8,760 hours.

Annual energy = Rated capacity × Hours per year × Capacity factor

Annual energy = 100 × 8,760 × 0.30

**Illustrative annual generation = 262,800 kWh**

This equals **262.8 MWh per year**.

The capacity factor in this example is an assumption for calculation purposes. It is not a prediction for a real site.

### Example: How Much Does the Wind Resource Matter?

Wind power available in the air is proportional to the cube of wind speed before turbine and system limitations are applied.

In simplified terms:

Available wind power ∝ Wind speed³.

For example, compare wind speeds of 5 m/s and 6 m/s.

The ratio of available wind power is:

(6 ÷ 5)³ = 1.728.

This means the available power in the wind stream at 6 m/s is approximately **72.8% greater** than at 5 m/s, assuming the same rotor area and air density.

This does not mean that a real turbine's electrical output will necessarily increase by 72.8%. Its power curve, control system, operating limits, and local conditions determine actual output.

The example demonstrates why accurate wind measurements matter.

### Example: Estimating Electricity Value

Assume the 100 kW turbine generates 262,800 kWh per year and 80% of this generation offsets electricity that would otherwise be purchased.

Energy offset = 262,800 × 0.80

**Energy offset = 210,240 kWh per year**

At an assumed avoided electricity cost of ₹7 per kWh:

210,240 × ₹7 = **₹14,71,680 per year**.

This is an illustrative value of electricity offset before project costs. It excludes the value of the remaining generation and does not account for maintenance, financing, taxes, capital expenditure, or uncertainty in the resource estimate.

### What Should a Wind Assessment Report Include?

Depending on the agreed scope, a report may include:

- Site and project description.
- Available wind data and its limitations.
- Wind-speed and wind-direction analysis.
- Terrain and obstacle observations.
- Candidate turbine specifications.
- Power-curve-based energy estimates.
- Estimated gross and net annual energy production.
- Relevant technical and operational assumptions.
- Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis.
- Preliminary system configuration.
- Indicative project economics.
- Recommended next steps.

For formal turbine power-performance testing, IEC 61400-12-1 provides a recognised methodology. The appropriate standards and measurement methods depend on the project and assessment objective.

### Who Should Consider This Service?

Wind resource assessment is relevant to property owners, industrial facilities, farmers, developers, institutions, infrastructure operators, and investors considering a new wind installation.

It is especially important when a project has significant capital costs or when financial decisions depend on achieving a particular annual energy yield.

# 5. Off-grid Wind for Remote Sites

## Wind Energy for Locations Beyond Reliable Grid Access

### Overview

Remote facilities may need electricity where utility-grid access is unavailable, unreliable, or expensive to extend.

Examples include remote monitoring stations, telecommunications infrastructure, isolated agricultural facilities, environmental research sites, border infrastructure, and certain rural installations.

A wind energy system can potentially contribute to these facilities' electricity needs where the wind resource is sufficient.

Because wind output varies with weather, an off-grid system must be designed around the actual load and the availability of other energy sources. Batteries, solar PV, backup generation, or other forms of storage may be required to maintain continuity of supply.

AeroSun Energy can help evaluate the energy requirements and potential role of wind generation in an off-grid power system.

### How an Off-grid Wind System Works

A typical off-grid arrangement may include:

- One or more wind turbines.
- Turbine controller and compatible power electronics.
- Battery storage, if required.
- Inverter and charge-control equipment appropriate to the system.
- Electrical distribution and protective devices.
- Load monitoring and energy-management controls.
- Optional solar PV.
- Optional backup generator.
- Remote monitoring and communications equipment.

The system must be engineered to accommodate variable generation, battery charging limits, load peaks, low-wind periods, and high-wind protection.

A turbine cannot guarantee uninterrupted electricity simply because it is rated for a particular power output.

### Applications

**Telecommunications sites**

Wind generation may contribute to the electricity requirements of remote communications equipment, subject to a suitable resource and reliability design.

**Environmental monitoring stations**

Small electrical loads, data loggers, sensors, and communications equipment may be supplied by an appropriately sized renewable-energy system.

**Remote agricultural facilities**

Wind may support water pumping, lighting, equipment charging, and other compatible loads.

**Research stations**

A wind system can contribute to a hybrid energy supply for remote monitoring and research infrastructure.

**Isolated buildings and facilities**

Wind can be considered alongside solar PV, batteries, and backup supply where grid extension is not practical.

**Remote security and infrastructure installations**

Wind may contribute to power for surveillance, communications, and monitoring equipment, depending on the load profile and site conditions.

### Benefits

**Reduced dependence on grid access**

A suitable off-grid system can generate electricity without requiring a conventional grid connection.

**Energy-source diversification**

Wind can complement solar PV or other sources where the respective resource patterns are favourable.

**Potential reduction in fuel use**

Where wind energy displaces diesel generation, it may reduce fuel consumption and generator operating hours.

**Remote monitoring opportunities**

System controls can record battery condition, renewable-energy generation, electrical consumption, and faults.

**Adaptable system configuration**

The system can be designed around measured loads and site conditions, rather than using a one-size-fits-all package.

### Example Calculation: A Remote Monitoring Station

Consider a remote station with an average electrical load of 1 kW.

Assume:

- Average continuous load: 1 kW.
- Daily energy requirement: 24 hours.
- Annual energy requirement: 365 days.

Daily energy consumption = 1 × 24

**Daily demand = 24 kWh**

Annual energy consumption = 24 × 365

**Annual demand = 8,760 kWh**

Now consider a hypothetical 5 kW wind turbine with an illustrative capacity factor of 30%.

Annual wind generation = 5 × 8,760 × 0.30

**Estimated annual wind generation = 13,140 kWh**

The average generation under this assumption is:

13,140 ÷ 8,760 = **1.5 kW average**.

On an annual-energy basis, this is greater than the station's average 1 kW load.

However, annual generation alone cannot establish whether the system will reliably supply the station. There may be periods when the wind turbine produces little or no electricity.

The design must therefore evaluate:

- Hourly or sub-hourly load variation.
- Wind-speed distribution and low-wind periods.
- Battery capacity and usable depth of discharge.
- Inverter efficiency and surge capability.
- Turbine cut-in and cut-out behaviour.
- Backup requirements.
- Required autonomy during low-generation periods.
- Maintenance access and equipment availability.

### Example: Preliminary Battery Sizing

Suppose the station requires 24 kWh per day and the design objective is to provide two days of autonomy using batteries.

Required usable energy:

24 × 2 = **48 kWh usable storage**.

If the selected battery system is designed around an illustrative usable fraction of 80%, the nominal battery energy required would be:

48 ÷ 0.80 = **60 kWh nominal battery capacity**.

This is a simplified energy calculation, not a final battery specification.

The final design must account for battery chemistry, temperature, ageing, discharge limits, inverter losses, peak loads, charging efficiency, and the required reliability standard.

### Wind + Solar + Battery Hybrid Systems

For many remote applications, a hybrid system may be more practical than relying on wind alone.

A hybrid configuration may include:

- Wind turbines for wind-dependent generation.
- Solar PV for daylight generation.
- Batteries for energy storage.
- An inverter and control system.
- A backup generator, where justified.
- Remote monitoring and alarms.

For example, a remote facility could use solar generation during the day, wind generation when the wind resource is available, and batteries to supply the load during periods of insufficient generation.

The value of this arrangement depends on the actual overlap between the renewable resources and the site's electricity demand.

### Who Should Consider This Solution?

This service may be relevant to telecommunications operators, remote facilities, research stations, agricultural operations, and infrastructure owners that require electricity away from a reliable grid connection.

An off-grid project should begin with a load assessment and resource evaluation. In some locations, solar-plus-battery may be simpler and more economical than a wind-based system.

# 6. Wind Farms & Grid Integration

## Connecting Wind Generation to the Electrical Grid

### Overview

Wind farms generate electricity from multiple wind turbines and deliver that electricity through a collection network, electrical substations, and an appropriate grid interconnection.

A wind farm may supply a utility network, support an industrial or commercial power arrangement, or form part of a broader renewable-energy portfolio.

Successful grid integration requires more than installing turbines. It involves wind-resource assessment, turbine selection, electrical engineering, power-quality management, protection, forecasting, grid compliance, and coordination with the relevant utility or system operator.

AeroSun Energy can discuss wind project concepts and the planning considerations involved in connecting wind generation with electrical infrastructure.

The detailed development and implementation of a wind farm require appropriately qualified engineering teams, project-specific studies, applicable approvals, and compliance with the relevant grid requirements.

### Main Components of a Wind Farm

**Wind turbines**

Convert wind energy into electrical power.

**Foundations and access infrastructure**

Support turbines and provide access for construction, operation, and maintenance.

**Turbine transformers**

Raise the voltage of generated electricity where required by the collection-system design.

**Collector network**

Connects individual turbines to the project's electrical substation.

**Substation and switchgear**

Provide voltage transformation, switching, protection, and other electrical functions.

**Protection and control systems**

Help detect faults and ensure the installation operates within defined limits.

**Grid interconnection**

Connects the wind farm to the relevant transmission or distribution network under approved technical arrangements.

**Monitoring and supervisory control**

Support operational monitoring, alarms, data collection, and control.

**Forecasting and energy management**

Help operators anticipate generation changes and coordinate with the electrical network.

**Optional battery storage**

May support selected applications such as ramp-rate management, energy shifting, or other grid services where technically and commercially justified.

### Applications

**Utility-scale electricity generation**

Wind farms can contribute electricity to the broader power network.

**Industrial and commercial renewable-energy procurement**

Wind power may be part of an eligible electricity-supply arrangement for large consumers.

**Renewable-energy portfolios**

### Example Calculation: A 20 MW Wind Farm

Consider a hypothetical wind farm with:

- Total installed capacity: 20 MW.
- Number of turbines: 10.
- Rated capacity per turbine: 2 MW.
- Illustrative capacity factor: 35%.
- Hours per year: 8,760.

Total installed capacity:

10 × 2 MW = **20 MW**.

Annual energy generation:

20 MW × 8,760 hours × 0.35

**Estimated annual generation = 61,320 MWh**.

This equals **61.32 GWh per year**.

Average power output:

20 MW × 0.35 = **7 MW average**.

The wind farm has a rated capacity of 20 MW, but the illustrative average output is 7 MW under the assumed capacity factor.

The actual output varies with wind conditions, turbine availability, electrical losses, curtailment, and other factors.

### Example: Estimating Gross Electricity Revenue

Assume, purely for illustration, that the project sells 61,320 MWh of electricity at an average realised price of ₹4 per kWh.

Convert energy to kWh:

61,320 MWh × 1,000 = 61,320,000 kWh.

Gross electricity revenue:

61,320,000 × ₹4

**Illustrative gross annual revenue = ₹24,52,80,000**, or approximately **₹24.53 crore**.

This is a simplified gross-revenue example, not a revenue forecast or profitability estimate.

It excludes capital expenditure, financing, land costs, operations and maintenance, insurance, transmission and wheeling charges, losses, curtailment, taxes, contractual adjustments, and other project expenses.

The assumed ₹4 per kWh is not a market quotation or a statement of any current electricity tariff.

# Frequently Asked Questions

## 1. Can a wind turbine generate electricity at low wind speeds?

Some turbines begin generating electricity at relatively low wind speeds, but cut-in wind speed alone does not indicate how much energy a turbine will produce.

A turbine may rotate without producing meaningful usable electricity. The most useful comparison is its documented power curve combined with the site's wind-speed distribution.

## 2. Which is better: a horizontal-axis or vertical-axis wind turbine?

Neither design is universally better.

Horizontal-axis turbines are widely used in commercial wind generation. Vertical-axis turbines may suit selected applications, but their performance, structural requirements, turbulence tolerance, maintenance needs, and economics vary by design.

The right selection depends on the intended application and measured site conditions.

## 3. Can wind turbines be installed on rooftops?

Potentially, yes, but rooftop installation requires careful assessment of wind turbulence, structural capacity, vibration, noise, blade clearance, maintenance access, and applicable building requirements.

A rooftop installation should not proceed solely because there is sufficient physical space.

## 4. Can a wind turbine power an irrigation pump?

Potentially, yes. The turbine and electrical system must be designed around the pump's energy consumption, starting requirements, operating schedule, and water demand.

Battery storage, water storage, solar PV, grid power, or backup generation may be needed to maintain the required pumping service.

## 5. Can an off-grid wind system supply electricity continuously?

Wind alone cannot guarantee continuous supply because wind speeds vary.

Reliable off-grid systems may require appropriately sized batteries, solar PV, backup generation, load management, or other measures based on the required reliability level.

## 6. How much electricity can a wind turbine generate per day?

There is no universal daily figure.

A preliminary estimate can be calculated using:

**Daily average energy = Rated capacity × 24 hours × Capacity factor**

For example, a 5 kW turbine with an assumed 30% capacity factor would generate:

5 × 24 × 0.30 = **36 kWh per day on average**.

This is a mathematical illustration, not a guaranteed daily output. Actual generation varies from day to day and may be substantially lower or higher.

## 7. How do I calculate potential electricity savings?

A simplified calculation is:

**Potential electricity value = Energy that offsets purchased electricity × Avoided electricity cost per kWh**

For example, if 10,000 kWh of wind generation actually offsets purchased electricity at ₹7 per kWh:

10,000 × ₹7 = **₹70,000 in gross annual electricity value**.

This does not represent net savings. Maintenance, capital cost, financing, losses, and other expenses must also be considered.

## 8. Is wind energy suitable for every location?

No. Wind energy depends strongly on the local resource and installation conditions.

A site assessment should consider wind speed, wind direction, turbulence, obstacles, terrain, turbine specifications, electrical demand, and project economics before a system is selected.

## 9. Can wind and solar operate together?

Yes. Wind and solar can be integrated into a compatible hybrid system. The design may include shared power conversion, battery storage, energy-management controls, and grid or backup connections.

The system must be engineered so that each source operates within its electrical and operational limits.

## 10. Do I need a wind resource assessment before buying a turbine?

For projects where performance and financial returns matter, site assessment is strongly recommended.

The required level of assessment depends on project scale, site complexity, and investment risk. A preliminary screening may be sufficient to reject an unsuitable site, while larger projects may require detailed measurement and energy-yield studies.
